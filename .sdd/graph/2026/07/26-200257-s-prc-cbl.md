Adversarial review applied round after round to the same implementation reproduces pre-flight's find-something bias at the code level: a reviewer prompted to find something will find something, and when each finding is accepted as an "implementation fix requiring no decision," the fixes compound into defensive machinery nobody decided to build. Observed during the session branch-binding implementation and quantified by its merge-readiness evaluation (20260726-195621-s-tac-0hg): the plan-level gate held perfectly — every explicitly rejected mechanism (coexistence protocols, authority registries, established-repo-id rekey) stayed out, and every new policy question was stopped and escalated to dialogue — yet the code-level output still landed at four to five times its warranted size, because individually-reasonable defensive fixes (symlink-rebind TOCTOU hardening, per-payload transaction state machines, rollback of already-byte-verified publishes) faced no proportionality check. The two gates are different in kind: the scope gate asks "was this decided?", which a finding can be tested against mechanically; a proportionality gate must ask "is this fix's threat model credible here, and is its weight proportionate to the scenario?", which requires judging the fix, not just the finding. The empirical pre-flight corpus (20260415-112708-s-prc-dix) documented the same dynamic in validation — roughly 35–57% false positives under find-something pressure, with defensive over-application among the named anti-patterns. The countermeasures that worked in this episode, worth carrying forward: treat repeated adversarial rounds that each mint new plan-level or defensive work as the signal to stop reviewing and land, and accept a defensive addition only against an explicitly stated threat model and a proportionate-size estimate — never as free.
