A rule and a guiding directive are distinguished not by being situational-vs-always-on (every entry is scoped to some area) but by whether the guidance carries an activation — a "when". A rule is shaped "when [recognizable recurring situation], consider / check / enforce [X]" (d-cpt-30v's self-describing when-to-apply / what-to-consider / how-to-calibrate): it fires at a juncture, is matched by that situation, and can be checked — binding blocks, advisory surfaces. A guiding directive is a persistent stance over an area — "[X] holds / we lean [X]" — with no triggering moment and nothing to fire or check; you reason in alignment with it, and its "when" is merely "always, while working in this area," a scope rather than a trigger. The two layer rather than compete: a directive sets the stance for an area, and rules are the checkable, juncture-firing constraints under it (the "output is dual-audience" stance versus the "honor NO_COLOR" rule). This sharpens the rule-vs-directive discriminator the prototype evaluation left as situational-vs-always-on (s-cpt-7ca), and is orthogonal to the development-vs-base-process axis (d-cpt-dym): the two axes together place an entry. A corollary resolves the always-loaded-context scaling worry: held to this line, guiding directives are a small set of broad area-stances — cheap to surface, lightly topic/layer-scoped — while the many specific checkable constraints are rules discovered by their activation; unbounded growth of the always-loaded guiding set is the smell of situational rules mis-filed as stances. The rule plan (d-tac-eho) must honor this boundary, or building the rule kind re-creates the contract-overload confusion it set out to resolve.
