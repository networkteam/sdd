The `/sdd` skill has grown through accumulated playbook moves and detailed guidance to a size where modularization options are worth weighing. The augment-plan pattern (d-prc-9ti) added ~50 lines today; earlier additions include the catch-up, explore, and grooming playbooks, language guidance, sync-check guidance, actor/role rules, and capture discipline.

Two grounded concerns shape the question:

- **Context cost**: per the token measurement (s-tac-n8u), the skill-metadata trigger surface is small (~1.2k for all SDD-family skills combined) but the active-skill load is meaningful when `/sdd` invokes — moderate at 1m context, larger proportionally at 200k.
- **Attention dilution**: per the research scan (s-cpt-0db), instruction-count degradation is empirically measured (ManyIFEval, AgentIF), and lost-in-the-middle persists on long-context models. The risk is empirical, not theoretical.

Two candidate modularization directions:

1. **Reference files loaded on demand** — extract playbook content into `references/<playbook>.md` and have the SKILL.md retain short triggers, extending the existing pattern for framework-concepts/meta-process/cli-reference.
2. **Sub-skills with their own triggers** — extract playbook moves into separate skills, following the precedent of `/sdd-bootstrap`, `/sdd-explore`, `/sdd-groom`, `/sdd-catchup`.

Design implication grounded in s-cpt-0db: keep invariant high-importance constraints (capture discipline, type/layer rules, canonical names) in the always-loaded surface; move domain-specific playbook moves and edge-case workflows to on-demand references or sub-skills.

Approach to resolve: implement a candidate modularization on a branch, run comparative scenario-based evaluation (capture flows, mode-selection cues, playbook adherence, structural-instruction adherence) against both versions, score against an expected-behavior rubric. There is no off-the-shelf benchmark for the precise question; the practical path is a small custom rubric on representative scenarios. Routing reliability is the main risk — per s-stg-3vr, text instructions don't reliably control agent behavior — so the eval must specifically test whether the parent skill triggers the right module.
