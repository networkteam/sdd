Surfaces that name an entry in passing — a card in a lineage view, a hover gloss on an entry ID, a row in a list — have only the summary as the shortest readable description of what the entry is about, and even the summary's first sentence is too much there to give a reader a quick handle on the entry.

Observed while building and reviewing clickable mockups of a web UI for browsing a real graph: cards, ID popovers and lineage lists rendered the first summary sentence, and in every layout tried these sentences crowded the views until they read as overwhelming, pushing back the content the reader came for. Cutting the sentence short does not help: the fragment keeps the summary's lead-in ("This plan commits …") and loses the subject. Short titles written as stand-ins for the mockups made it much easier to see what each entry is about.

The opposite expectation is on record twice. The hover-gloss insight (20260625-143326-s-cpt-9dp) took the summary's first sentence as the natural gloss unit, and the summary directive (20260901-112834-d-cpt-75a) holds the minimized first sentence to be the compact identity form dense surfaces need — on that ground it closed the earlier proposal for a short summary field (20260517-180646-s-cpt-c43). For human reading surfaces the mockups contradict that: a sentence that states what an entry observes, commits to or did is still a sentence, not a handle.

Candidate direction, not decided: a short title per entry, kept in frontmatter like the summary field — backfilled for existing entries, crafted together with the body when a new entry is captured, and correctable later the way a summary is, without rewriting the entry. Unlike the closed proposal's generated second summary, a new entry's title would be authored with it — whether that escapes the directive's drift objection is part of the open question. Writing a good compressed title is hard: it needs reliable guidance on what a good title is and what is expected of it, which needs its own design session.
